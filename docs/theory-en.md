# Theory: what the estimates mean

WLVDB asks how much labour is required to reproduce the commodities that an
economy produces, how that labour relates to workers' consumption, and how
international trade redistributes the value it represents. Answering requires
both a theory of what is being measured and a model connecting that theory to
observed production, employment and expenditure.

Read this chapter in order on a first visit. It explains the concepts before
the [mathematical chapter](methodology-en.md) derives the calculations. The
current `wiodr13` and `wiodr16` methods are particular implementations of the
research programme of Franklin et al. (2022) and Franklin (2025). They do not
implement every alternative discussed in those works, and they do not automatically
reproduce their published tables.

## 1. From useful things to social labour

Bread can feed someone; a machine can shape metal; a transport service can
move a product to where it is needed. These are **use-values**: useful
qualities of goods or services. The useful object and the activity producing
it are concrete and particular. An hour of baking differs from an hour of
engineering in what the worker does and what the result can do.

In a commodity economy, these different products are also exchanged. Their
**exchange-value** is a relation in which one commodity exchanges for another;
a **price** expresses such a relation in money. Marx's concept of **value**
concerns the social labour represented by a commodity. Its magnitude is
determined by **socially necessary labour time**: labour under socially
prevailing conditions of production, with normal skill and intensity. A
producer taking twice as long because of an inefficient technique does not
thereby create twice the value of the same commodity. This theoretical
distinction is the starting point of the empirical approach, rather than a
result generated by the software
([Franklin et al., 2022, p. 365–370](references-en.md#franklin-et-al-2022)).

**Concrete labour** names labour in its particular useful form. **Abstract
labour** names the common social dimension through which the products of
different activities become comparable. The latter is not a claim that
workers, skills or working conditions are physically identical. Nor can a
matrix alone resolve how private work becomes socially validated.

The database uses country-industry accounts and reported hours to estimate
these magnitudes. Industry averages stand in for conditions of production;
the selected labour multiplier stands in for the reduction of heterogeneous
hours to a common unit. This is an empirical approximation. Differences
between firms, product quality, intensity, unsold production and skill can
remain hidden by aggregation. Thus `abstract_labour` is the method's
operational measure, not a direct statistical observation of abstract labour.

## 2. Why the whole production chain matters

A loaf of bread requires more labour than the baker's hours. It also requires
the labour associated with flour, electricity and the portion of equipment
used up in baking. Flour in turn requires grain, transport and processing.
Stopping at the last establishment would miss these requirements.

The model therefore separates three components:

| Component | Economic interpretation | Connection to the calculation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direct living labour | Labour performed during the current production process. | Direct hours divided by the industry's gross output. |
| Intermediate inputs | Products consumed while producing the current output. | Interindustry deliveries divided by purchaser output. |
| Depreciation of fixed capital | The portion of long-lived assets consumed during the period. | An estimated depreciation matrix divided by purchaser output. |

Adding direct labour to the labour required by inputs and depreciation yields
the commodity's total labour requirement. In Marxian terms, living labour
creates **new value**, while means of production transfer value through their
consumption. A machine's full stock does not enter every year's output: the
model uses the portion depreciated. These are the components of equation 1
in [Franklin et al. (2022, p. 370)](references-en.md#franklin-et-al-2022).

The distinction also prevents double counting. Suppose flour embodies 2
hours and the baker adds 1 hour. Bread embodies 3 hours. Adding the flour's
2 hours to the bread's 3 hours gives a gross-output total of 5 hours, but only
3 hours are required for the final bread across its production chain. An embodied gross-output measure
and a new-value measure answer different questions. The units can both be
hours while the economic content differs.

In WLVDB, the matrix indicator `values` converts deliveries into embodied
hours; `value.m.mv` gives labour requirements per monetary unit of output.
`abstract_labour` measures direct weighted labour. Consult the
[dictionary](results-dictionary-en.md) for the precise population, unit and
aggregation of each published indicator.

## 3. Why monetary input-output accounts can be used

An input-output table records who supplies each product and who uses it.
The supplying industry occupies the row; the purchasing industry occupies
the column. When a purchaser uses an input, the model also needs the labour
required to supply that input. Repeating this reasoning connects every stage
of production, including stages located abroad
([Franklin, 2025, p. 190–195](references-en.md#franklin-2025)).

Monetary records are usable because money can serve as a unit in which to
express product quantities. Suppose the same basket is priced at USD 10 and
requires 2 hours. Its coefficient is 0.2 hour/USD. If that basket is priced
at USD 20 while its physical requirements are unchanged, the coefficient is
0.1 hour/USD. Multiplying each coefficient by its corresponding monetary
flow returns the same 2 hours. This example changes the unit in a consistent
homogeneous model; it is not a claim that real-world price movements leave
production or distribution unchanged.

The procedure therefore **does not require observed prices to equal labour
values**. Monetary output and input flows are combined with independently
reported hours; the resulting coefficient measures hours per monetary unit,
not money per hour worked. The algebraic change of units is demonstrated in
the [mathematical chapter](methodology-en.md). The article explicitly notes
that output can be expressed in physical or monetary units
([Franklin et al., 2022, p. 386, note 16](references-en.md#franklin-et-al-2022)).

This reasoning requires compatible valuations, a common industry structure,
and a usable approximation to the products and techniques within each
industry. A dollar's worth of a sector's product is treated as requiring the
same production structure regardless of the buyer. Different product mixes,
buyer-specific prices, margins, taxes or inconsistent statistical boundaries
can weaken that approximation. A method that converts dollars to hours must
document these limitations as well as the arithmetic.

The **Leontief system** is the set of these mutually dependent production
requirements. Under the relevant convergence conditions, it adds the direct,
indirect and successively more remote requirements. Solving it establishes
consistency with the chosen accounts and assumptions. It does not by itself
establish that those assumptions describe every economic process correctly.

## 4. Productive and unproductive labour

The productive/unproductive distinction concerns the role an activity plays
in the production and distribution of value. It is not a ranking of a
worker's effort, usefulness, dignity or entitlement to remuneration. An
activity can be indispensable to the operation of capitalism without being
treated as creating new value in the model.

The book uses a broad concept of productive labour associated with producing
or preserving a use-value in a commodity, because national economies also
contain commodity production outside capitalist wage employment. It
distinguishes this from the narrower definition of labour producing
surplus value for capital
([Franklin, 2025, p. 193–194, note 74](references-en.md#franklin-2025)).

Neither definition identifies productive labour simply with making a
tangible object. Services can enter the productive category. Conversely,
selling or allocating claims on existing wealth need not create a new
use-value. Applying these distinctions to industries that combine several
activities requires judgement and invites debate.

WLVDB makes that judgement explicit in the `productive` column of
[`wiodr13/_sectors.csv`](../methods/wiodr13/_sectors.csv) and
[`wiodr16/_sectors.csv`](../methods/wiodr16/_sectors.csv). The present
labour-requirement system uses the productive block and assigns structural
zeros outside it. Such a zero is a model classification: it does not mean
that nobody worked or that no payment occurred. Compensation, employment
and transactions in those industries remain economically relevant.

The classifications also have a history. The 2022 article lists hotels and
restaurants and other community, social and personal services among its
unproductive industries; the current WIOD13 sector file marks both as
productive. The article's classification cannot substitute for checking the
versioned file used by a run
([Franklin et al., 2022, p. 386–387, note 21](references-en.md#franklin-et-al-2022)).

## 5. Labour-power, wages and surplus value

A worker sells **labour-power**, the capacity to work. Maintaining and
reproducing that capacity requires food, housing, care, education and other
historically and socially determined conditions. The labour required to
produce its means of reproduction is the **value of labour-power**. It is
conceptually different from the value the worker creates during the working
day. Training costs can affect reproduction costs without determining an
identical increase in the value created per working hour
([Franklin et al., 2022, p. 365–366](references-en.md#franklin-et-al-2022)).

Suppose 8 hours of productive labour create new value and the means of
reproducing the corresponding labour-power require 3 hours. The remaining
5 hours are **surplus value**; the ratio of surplus to necessary labour is
`5 / 3`, approximately 167%. This is the **rate of exploitation**, also called
the rate of surplus value. It is not 5 divided by the entire 8-hour day, and
it is not a ratio between hours and wages in dollars.

To connect this idea to accounts, WLVDB estimates the labour content of a
consumption basket financed by compensation. It uses national household
consumption composition as a proxy for the composition of workers' purchases.
Multiplying expenditure shares by product labour requirements translates
money wages into basket hours. The resulting `labour_force_value` makes
the two sides of the comparison commensurable. The indicator family named
`surplus_value` reports the corresponding rate; the exact employment and
productive/unproductive coverage must be checked in the dictionary.

The proxy does not establish that actual compensation purchases all the
means socially necessary for reproduction. Household totals include
households with different income sources, and workers within a country
consume different baskets. The article uses national baskets for its
non-wage-based alternatives because worker- and industry-specific data were
unavailable ([Franklin et al., 2022, p. 374](references-en.md#franklin-et-al-2022)).

Nor does a high exploitation rate alone identify **superexploitation**.
That question concerns whether reproduction is impaired, including the
relationship between compensation, consumption and wear of labour-power.
It requires additional evidence on working time, intensity, living conditions
and reproduction needs. A rise in exploitation may accompany cheaper means
of subsistence and increased real consumption. The book examines precisely
why these questions must be distinguished
([Franklin, 2025, p. 243–248](references-en.md#franklin-2025)).

## 6. Why higher wages are not automatically more abstract labour

Reported hours differ in skill and intensity. Reducing them to a common unit
is therefore a substantive theoretical and empirical problem. One possible
shortcut gives greater weight to the hours of higher-paid workers. The
article examines why this shortcut can build the answer into the premise.

For relative wages to represent relative value creation, Ochoa's approach
requires a common consumption-basket structure, a uniform exploitation rate
and labour-market equilibrium. Yet wages are also shaped by class struggle,
discrimination, migration barriers and access to education. Workers compare
wages, rather than moving freely until their employers appropriate the same
proportion of value. If a wage-based multiplier imposes equal exploitation,
the resulting estimates cannot then independently test whether exploitation
is equal ([Franklin et al., 2022, p. 365–369](references-en.md#franklin-et-al-2022)).

| Approach discussed in the article | How hours are weighted | Main interpretation issue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ochoa 1 | Industry hourly remuneration relative to the lowest worldwide. | Links international wage gaps directly to value creation. |
| Ochoa 2 | Industry hourly remuneration relative to the lowest within each country. | National wage dispersion affects international comparisons. |
| Petrović | Labour shares by skill, weighted by average remuneration across skill strata. | Recognizes skill mix within industries, but retains wage-based assumptions. |
| Alternative 1 | All hours receive weight 1. | Does not measure differences in skill or intensity. |
| Alternative 2 | High-, medium- and low-skill hours receive weights 6.25, 2.5 and 1. | Illustrative arbitrary weights, not independently measured multipliers. |

The article compares all five approaches; **the currently executable WIOD
methods use equal hour weights**. They do not expose the five alternatives
as interchangeable command-line options. Equal weights are a parsimonious
choice in the absence of an independent measure, not a demonstration that
skill differences do not matter. The article finds similar movements under
its two non-wage-based alternatives, while acknowledging differences in
magnitudes and the unresolved need for better multipliers
([Franklin et al., 2022, p. 371–372, 383–384](references-en.md#franklin-et-al-2022)).

## 7. Prices, appropriation and international trade

A **direct price** is a price proportional to the estimated labour value,
expressed in money through a chosen normalization. It is an analytical
comparison with the market price, not a second observed selling price.
The current normalization uses world gross output. It is distinct from a
**price of production**, a theoretical price related to profit-rate
equalization, whose calculation is not implemented as a current WIOD output.
The article presents the distinction between values, direct prices and
production prices ([Franklin et al., 2022, p. 363, 370](references-en.md#franklin-et-al-2022)).

Because prices can differ from values, the surplus value produced in an
industry need not equal the profit appropriated there. Labour performed in
one place can sustain income appropriated elsewhere. The database's
appropriated-profit calculation is an accounting measure based on capital
compensation, depreciation and capital stock; it should not be relabelled as
surplus value divided by constant plus variable capital.

International trade makes this distinction visible. If one country sells
commodities containing 100 hours for USD 100 and buys commodities containing
150 hours for the same USD 100, its monetary trade is balanced, but it
receives 50 more embodied hours than it sends. This is an illustrative
case of unequal exchange under the model.

Actual monetary trade is often unbalanced. A country can import additional
embodied hours by running a monetary deficit. WLVDB therefore compares
embodied labour with the labour represented by the payment, using a common
annual benchmark from productive world trade. It accounts for both the
export and import legs; a positive national transfer balance denotes net
receipt. The [mathematical chapter](methodology-en.md) derives the correction
and explains the bilateral sign convention
([Franklin, 2025, p. 195–200](references-en.md#franklin-2025)).

This trade benchmark is distinct from the direct-price normalization of
world gross output. It is also distinct from a purchasing-power-parity
measure of the cost of living. None of these factors can be substituted
for another merely because it relates hours, money or purchasing power.

The transfer estimate alone does not identify whether wages, productivity,
monopoly, exchange rates or other mechanisms explain the observed result.
Nor does it measure every international appropriation channel. The book's
broader account of dependency also considers profits, interest, rents,
subordination and the connection between countries' accumulation processes.
Trade indicators are evidence for one part of that investigation
([Franklin, 2025, p. 135–208, 209–239](references-en.md#franklin-2025)).

## 8. What makes an estimate defensible

Four different questions need an answer:

1. **Does the concept fit the research question?** Embodied labour, new
   value, labour-power reproduction, profit and trade transfers are related
   but distinct objects.
2. **Does the statistical proxy fit the concept?** Check productive scope,
   employment populations, consumption baskets, imputations and aggregation.
3. **Does the calculation faithfully implement the model?** Units, matrix
   orientation, identities, convergence and numerical residuals must agree.
4. **Does the interpretation survive plausible alternatives?** Assess the
   sensitivity to classifications, capital estimates, labour reduction and
   incomplete country coverage before drawing substantive conclusions.

Passing the third question is necessary and valuable; it does not answer the
other three. A high price-value correlation likewise cannot by itself settle
the reduction problem or prove every theoretical claim. The purpose of open
code, explicit hypotheses and traceable sources is to make these judgements
possible. Continue with [the calculations](methodology-en.md),
[assumptions and research priorities](assumptions-en.md), and
[using, reproducing or extending the results](guide-en.md).
